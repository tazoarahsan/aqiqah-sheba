The birth of a child is one of the greatest blessings Allah bestows on a family, and Islam gives parents a beautiful way to respond to it with gratitude: the aqiqah. This single act — a sacrifice offered on the newborn's behalf — carries thankfulness, protection, charity, and love all at once, and it revives a cherished practice of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. Yet many parents are unsure exactly what aqiqah is, whether it is obligatory, when to do it, and how. This complete guide answers all of that from the authentic sources.

## What Is Aqiqah?

Aqiqah is the sacrifice of a permissible animal — traditionally a sheep or goat — offered in gratitude to Allah for the birth of a child. The word itself comes from an Arabic root meaning "to cut," and in early usage it referred to the hair on a newborn's head that is shaved shortly after birth; over time it came to mean the sacrifice offered on that occasion.

At its heart, aqiqah is an act of worship performed *for* the child by their parents or guardian. It is one of the first things done to welcome a new soul into the Muslim community, and it is closely tied to two other Sunnah acts on the same day: naming the child and shaving the head. Together, these mark the child's arrival with worship, generosity, and du'a.

## Is Aqiqah Obligatory? The Ruling Across the Madhabs

One of the first questions parents ask is whether aqiqah is compulsory. The honest answer is that the scholars differ, though the differences are narrow and reassuring.

The majority — the **Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali** schools — hold that aqiqah is a *Sunnah mu'akkadah*, an emphasised and strongly recommended practice. This is stated by al-Nawawi in *al-Majmu'* (8/411) and Ibn Qudamah in *al-Mughni* (9/364), and it is the preponderant view because it reconciles the Prophet's ﷺ clear encouragement of aqiqah with the absence of any text declaring the one who leaves it to be sinful.

The **Hanafi** school, which predominates in Bangladesh and South Asia, holds on the well-known view of Imam Abu Hanifah that aqiqah is *mubah* — permissible and commendable — rather than a binding Sunnah. Many later Hanafi scholars gave it a higher standing: Imam al-Tahawi called it desirable, and Mawlana Abd al-Hai Lakhnawi agreed. A minority, notably the **Zahiri** school (Ibn Hazm), held it to be obligatory.

The practical takeaway across every school is the same: performing aqiqah is virtuous and rewarding, no school criticises the family who performs it, and — for the majority — no sin falls on a family genuinely unable to do so.

This single narration establishes the act, its timing, and its two companion Sunnahs of naming and shaving. Abu Dawud (2838) records it with very similar wording.

A second proof comes from Salman ibn Amir al-Dabbi (RA), recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, in which the Prophet ﷺ instructed that a sacrifice be made for the newborn and the harm removed from him. The Prophet ﷺ also put his teaching into practice, performing aqiqah for his own grandsons Hasan and Husayn (RA).

## Is Aqiqah Mentioned in the Quran?

A common and fair question is whether aqiqah appears in the Quran. In honesty, **aqiqah is not named in the Quran** — it is established entirely by the authentic Sunnah. The Quran's role here is foundational rather than specific: Allah commands the believers to take whatever the Messenger ﷺ gives them and to abstain from what he forbids (Surah al-Hashr 59:7), and it is on this basis that the Prophet's ﷺ practices, including aqiqah, become part of the religion. The Quran also frames children as a blessing and adornment of worldly life (Surah al-Kahf 18:46), giving context to why gratitude for them is expressed through an act of worship.

## When Should Aqiqah Be Performed?

The recommended time for aqiqah is the **seventh day** after birth, as stated explicitly in the hadith of Samurah (RA). The day of birth is counted as the first day, so a child born on a Saturday has their seventh day the following Friday.

If the seventh day is missed, the good news is that the Sunnah is not lost. Many scholars mention performing it on the fourteenth day, and then the twenty-first — though the specific narration behind that pattern (from Buraydah, RA) is weak, so it is guidance many follow rather than a firm proof. The stronger, majority position is simply that aqiqah may be performed later whenever the family is able, ideally before the child reaches puberty, and there is no sin in a reasonable delay.

## How Many Animals, and Which Kind?

The traditional practice is **two comparable animals for a boy and one for a girl**, taught by the Prophet ﷺ in the hadith of Umm Kurz (RA) and the narration of Aisha (RA). Where two are not possible for a boy, one suffices, and the aqiqah is still valid. The reports of the Prophet's ﷺ own aqiqah for Hasan and Husayn come in two wordings from Ibn Abbas (RA): Abu Dawud records a single ram for each, while an-Nasa'i records two rams for each, which al-Albani judged the more accurate wording. Scholars who hold that one animal suffices for a boy draw on the first wording; two remains the more complete practice.

The preferred animal is a sheep or goat, as that is what the Prophet ﷺ used. The majority of scholars also permit a cow or camel, which count as seven shares. In all cases the animal must be sound and healthy, of the appropriate age, and free of disqualifying defects — conditions the jurists largely drew from the rules of the Eid sacrifice.

## The Sunnah Acts That Accompany Aqiqah

Aqiqah rarely stands alone. On the same seventh-day occasion, the child is **named** and the **head is shaved**, and it is Sunnah to give the weight of the shaved hair in silver as charity — from what the Prophet ﷺ taught Fatimah (RA) regarding Hasan (RA). Soon after birth, *tahneek* is also practised: gently rubbing a tiny amount of softened date on the newborn's palate, a practice firmly established in both Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. These acts together wrap the child's first days in worship and blessing.

## What Happens to the Aqiqah Meat?

The meat is meant to spread benefit. The Sunnah is generous and flexible: the family may eat from it, gift some to relatives and neighbours, and give some to the poor. There is no fixed, obligatory proportion for aqiqah — the well-known "thirds" split is a helpful guideline drawn by analogy from the Eid sacrifice, not a binding rule. The meat may be given raw or cooked, and preparing it for a warm family gathering is a well-loved, praiseworthy practice.

## Aqiqah vs Qurbani

Aqiqah is sometimes confused with Qurbani (udhiyah), but they are distinct. Qurbani is tied to the days of Eid al-Adha and commemorates the sacrifice of Prophet Ibrahim (AS); it can be offered every year by those who are able. Aqiqah is tied to the birth of a child and is offered once for that child. Different reasons, same underlying spirit of devotion and charity.

## Why Aqiqah Matters

Aqiqah matters because it combines several of the values a parent hopes to instil for a lifetime. It is an act of **gratitude** for the gift of a child; a means of seeking **protection and blessing**, as the Prophet ﷺ linked the sacrifice to removing harm from the newborn; a way of **following his ﷺ example**; and an act of **charity** that feeds relatives and the poor. Beginning a child's life with these is a beautiful foundation.

## Performing Your Aqiqah: Yourself or Through a Service

Families can fulfil this Sunnah in two valid ways: performing the aqiqah themselves, or appointing a trustworthy party to carry it out on their behalf. Delegation is entirely legitimate in Islam, and for many families — especially those living abroad or without the facilities to slaughter at home — a [reputable service](/en/packages) that sources a healthy animal, performs genuine halal slaughter, and distributes the meat to the poor is a sound and convenient way to honour the Sunnah correctly.

## Conclusion

Aqiqah is a short, beautiful act with lasting weight: gratitude to Allah, protection and blessing for the child, obedience to the Prophet ﷺ, and generosity to the community — all offered at the very start of a new life. Whether performed on the seventh day at home or arranged through a trusted service, it is a Sunnah well worth fulfilling.
