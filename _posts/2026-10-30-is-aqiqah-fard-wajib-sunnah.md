One of the most common questions parents ask is whether aqiqah is compulsory. The reassuring answer is that the scholars differ, the differences are narrow, and no school blames the family who performs it. Here is a clear overview of each position. For the broader picture, see our [complete guide to aqiqah](/blog/aqiqah-complete-guide/).

## The Hanafi view: permissible and commendable

In the Hanafi school — dominant in Bangladesh and South Asia — the well-known view of Imam Abu Hanifah is that aqiqah is *mubah*: permissible and praiseworthy, rather than a binding Sunnah or an obligation. Performing it earns reward and revives the Sunnah; leaving it is not a sin. Many later Hanafi scholars gave it a higher standing: Imam al-Tahawi described it as desirable, and Mawlana Abd al-Hayy al-Lakhnawi agreed.

## The majority view: an emphasised Sunnah

The Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali schools hold that aqiqah is a *Sunnah mu'akkadah* — an emphasised, strongly recommended practice. This is the most widely held opinion because it fits the evidence neatly: the Prophet ﷺ clearly encouraged and performed aqiqah, yet there is no authentic text declaring the one who omits it to be sinful. It is recorded by al-Nawawi in *al-Majmu'* (8/411) and Ibn Qudamah in *al-Mughni* (9/364).

## The minority view: obligatory

A minority of scholars held aqiqah to be *wajib* (obligatory). This is the position of the Zahiri school, associated with Dawud al-Zahiri and Ibn Hazm, and a similar strong view is reported from al-Hasan al-Basri and al-Layth ibn Sa'd. They read the word "held in pledge" in the hadith above in its strongest sense.

## What this means for you

Whatever school you follow, the practical conclusion is the same: aqiqah is virtuous and encouraged, and performing it is always a good choice. As al-Shawkani summarised, aqiqah is among the Sunnahs of Islam. A family that is able should happily perform it; a family that cannot is not sinful on the view of the majority, and may do it later when able.
